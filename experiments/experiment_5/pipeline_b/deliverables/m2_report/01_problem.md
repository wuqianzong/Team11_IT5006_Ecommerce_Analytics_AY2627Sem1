## 1 Problem and stakeholders

Olist is a Brazilian marketplace: independent sellers ship each order to the customer through a carrier, and the customer reviews the purchase afterwards. Two things go wrong often enough to matter. Deliveries arrive later than the customer expected, and a share of customers leave a 1 or 2 star review, which hurts the seller's and the platform's reputation.

**Why it matters to the business.** The development period, January 2017 to late May 2018, holds {{B2.reg.dev_rows_A}} delivered orders, each worth on average R$ {{A3.reg.price_mean}} in goods plus R$ {{A3.reg.freight_mean}} in freight, so a customer lost to a poor experience is revenue lost to the seller and the platform. The business grew fast: monthly revenue from item prices rose from R$ {{BIZ.rev_2017_03}} in March 2017 to R$ {{BIZ.rev_2018_04}} in April 2018, {{BIZ.rev_growth}} times as much, with a peak of R$ {{BIZ.rev_peak}} in November 2017 against R$ {{BIZ.rev_2017_10}} in October (figure below). Delivery is where the experience breaks under pressure. When volume jumped in November 2017 (Black Friday: {{A3.trend.orders_2017_11}} orders against {{A3.trend.orders_2017_10}} in October), the share of orders delivered after Olist's promised date rose from {{A3.trend.late_2017_10}} to {{A3.trend.late_2017_11}}, and it peaked at {{A3.trend.late_2018_03}} in March 2018. Late orders drive bad reviews: {{A3.clf.detractor_rate_late}} of late orders received a 1 or 2 star review, against {{A3.clf.detractor_rate_on_time}} of orders delivered on time, and the detractor share rose from {{A3.trend.detractor_2017_10}} in October 2017 to {{A3.trend.detractor_2018_03}} in March 2018 ({{A3.clf.dev_prevalence}} over the whole period). Reviews are public, and a disappointed customer may not buy again. Promising faster is not a free fix either: in an experiment with an online retailer, faster delivery promises raised sales and profit but also reduced customer retention (Cui et al., 2024). Few customers order again soon: in a typical month only {{BIZ.repeat_mean}} of customers placed more than one order in that month (between {{BIZ.repeat_min}} and {{BIZ.repeat_max}}), so revenue rests on many occasional buyers, for whom one late delivery or one bad review can be the whole experience of the platform.

The two models answer these two problems. The lead-time model gives a delivery promise the operation can keep, so sales are not bought with broken promises; the detractor alert lets customer service reach an at-risk customer before the review is written. We frame one delivery experience as two prediction tasks, both made at checkout, when the order is placed.

*Table 1. The two prediction tasks.*

| | Regression: delivery lead time | Classification: detractor alert |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | How many days from purchase to delivery? | Will the customer leave a 1–2 star review? |
| Target | Lead time in days, purchase to delivery (modelled on the log scale) | Detractor: the order's lowest valid review score is 1 or 2 |
| Prediction moment | At checkout | At checkout |
| Who uses it, and how | Fulfilment and customer service show a delivery range to the customer when the order is placed | Customer service flags orders at risk when they are placed, for follow-up before delivery and review |
| Primary metric | MAE in days; RMSE, R² and the share within 3 days reported beside it | Average precision (AP); ROC-AUC, Brier score, precision, recall and F1 at the threshold reported beside it |
| Cost of an error | A late promise breaks the customer's expectation; an early one only looks pessimistic | One missed detractor is weighted as five false alerts (an assumption, used to set the threshold) |

**Why checkout.** At checkout the platform knows the basket, the prices, the seller and customer locations and the seller's past reviews, which is what a delivery promise can use, and an alert raised then leaves the longest time to act. Its limit is that nothing about this order's dispatch is known yet, although the strongest driver of a bad review is a late delivery; section 7 returns to this.

**Why MAE and AP.** MAE is measured in days, the unit of the promise, and is less driven by a few very late orders than RMSE, which we report alongside. Detractors are a minority of orders ({{M2.clf.test_prevalence_A}} of the test orders), so accuracy would look high for a model that never alerts: with imbalanced classes, accuracy can hide weak detection of the minority class (He and Garcia, 2009). AP summarises precision over all recall levels; a random ranking scores about the detractor share, so that share is the baseline AP is judged against. The cost weighting turns the ranking into a decision: the alert threshold is the probability above which an alert costs less than a likely missed detractor.

**How we judge success.** Each task is judged against a dummy model scored on the same test orders, which predicts the median lead time or the detractor share for every order: a useful model must beat it clearly. On the test, our lead-time model's MAE is {{M2.reg.test_mae_A}} days against {{M2.reg.test_mae_Adummy}} for the dummy, and our alert's AP is {{M2.clf.test_ap_A}} against a detractor share of {{M2.clf.test_prevalence_A}}. The rules for keeping a more complex model were fixed before the test was read; no business target in days or AP was set in advance, so the dummy is how we judge the result, not a promise made beforehand.

**Stakeholders.** Fulfilment owns the delivery promise; customer service owns the alert and the contact; seller management uses both to see which sellers and routes fall behind (section 7).

## 9 Business recommendations

The models serve one chain: the promise at checkout, the seller's dispatch, the carrier's transit and, if the order is still late, service recovery before the review. Test numbers are a post-test analysis of Pipeline B's {{BC.test_orders}} test orders (no refit); the {{B12.reg.buffer_90}}-day buffer chosen on validation remains the unbiased result. Tables K16 and K18 to K20 give the detail.

**1. Promise** (observation; literature-supported hypothesis for sales). Olist's own estimate promised a median of {{BC.promise.olist_median}} days, missed the actual lead time by {{BC.promise.olist_mae}} days on average, and was late for {{BC.promise.olist_late}} of orders; Pipeline B shown as a date is late for {{BC.promise.B_late}}. A buffer turns the prediction into a promise and works as a business dial (figure below, Table K18): plus 6 days promises {{BC.promise.B6_median}} days with {{BC.promise.B6_late}} late; plus 10 days matches Olist's length with {{BC.promise.B10_late}} late. We recommend an operational rule: each month, take the smallest buffer whose late share on recent weeks is at or below that of Olist's estimate; on the last validation fold this gives {{BC.buffer.recentolist_buffer_days}} days, without test data. The 12-day buffer is conservative because validation includes the late-2017 crisis, when Pipeline B without a buffer was late for {{BC.buffer.all_validation_share_late_no_buffer}} of orders, against {{BC.promise.B_late}} on the test. Kept, shorter promises are expected to help sales (Fisher et al., 2019; Salari et al., 2022), while over-promising has costs (Cui et al., 2024).

**2. Dispatch** (observation). Under that promise, {{BC.expedite.B10_share_of_late_with_late_handover}} of late orders left the seller after the shipping limit. A rule that flags missed dispatch has precision {{B9.clf.missed_dispatch_precision}} and catches {{B9.clf.missed_dispatch_share}} of all detractors, with no model. That working with slow sellers would cut late deliveries is an unmeasured mechanism.

**3. Transit** (scenario; unmeasured mechanism). Had transit been 10% faster for those late handovers, only {{BC.expedite.B10_share_of_all_late_on_time}} of late orders would have arrived on time, so most late orders need service recovery.

**4. Service recovery** (observation; operational rule for the workflow). Pipeline B at 4/15 sends {{B10.clf.B_4_15_test_alerts_day}} alerts a day at precision {{B10.clf.B_4_15_test_precision}}, reaching about {{BC.reach.B415_reached_day}} detractors a day; 4/15 is a capacity choice, and 1/6 is cost-optimal at 5:1. The fallback at 1/6 adds {{BC.reach.A16_alerts_day}} a day at precision {{BC.reach.A16_precision}}, below the missed-dispatch rule's, for a total of {{B10.clf.combined_test_alerts_day}} a day at precision {{B10.clf.combined_test_precision}}; a higher fallback threshold or the rule alone are options (Table K19). In the dashboard, customer service sets the threshold, works the daily queue (Pipeline B by risk, then the fallback by handover delay and order value), contacts the customer from the case file with a tracking update and a voucher for the next order, and passes the shortlist to seller management each week; the buffer is reset each month. The review cap of {{B10.clf.review_cap}} alerts a day applies to Pipeline B alone. A freight discount moves few still-late alerts below 4/15 ({{BC.discount.B10_f30_stops}} of {{BC.discount.B10_flagged}} at 30% off; Table K20), an association, not an effect.

**Cost** (assumption). Weighting a missed detractor as five false alerts, the serving rule's test cost is {{B10.clf.combined_test_cost_5_1}} ({{B10.clf.B_4_15_test_cost_5_1}} from Pipeline B, {{B10.clf.A_out_test_cost_5_1}} from the fallback). A contact pays when precision × the value kept per detractor reached exceeds its cost; both values are unknown, so a randomised hold-back pilot comes first (section 10).
